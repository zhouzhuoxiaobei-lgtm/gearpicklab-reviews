Glass construction matters more here than the listing's "quality materials" line suggests. Kombucha sits at a pH around 2.5 to 3.5 depending on how long it ferments. That's acidic enough to leach from reactive metals and to stain or retain odors in scratched plastic. Glass doesn't scratch under normal SCOBY handling - a cellulose mat is soft - and it won't react with the acetic and gluconic acids the culture produces. If you've ever brewed in a food-grade plastic bucket and noticed a persistent vinegar smell that survived washing, this is the difference. The trade-off is weight. A full 5-liter glass jar with liquid runs heavy. And the listing doesn't state an empty weight or a filled weight. Plan for a two-handed lift when you move it for cleaning. The stainless steel mesh lid is the other functional spec worth attention. Kombucha calls for oxygen during primary fermentation. But it also attracts fruit flies once the acetic acid develops. A mesh top lets air in and keeps insects out. That's a straightforward design choice with an obvious failure mode if the mesh is too fine - restricted airflow slows the culture. The listing doesn't specify mesh gauge. So I can't tell you how open the weave actually is. That's a gap worth noting if you're comparing this against a cloth-and-rubber-band configuration. The spigot is the feature that justifies the whole format. Continuous brewing works because you're drawing finished kombucha from the bottom of the vessel. Where it's most acidic and least sweet, while the fresh tea you add floats on top and ferments gradually. The spigot makes that draw-off a thirty-second task instead of a siphon-and-mess operation.
