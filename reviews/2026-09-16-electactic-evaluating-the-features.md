 What the specs mean in practice The 15-bar pump is the standard figure for home espresso machines in this class. Bar pressure above the roughly 9 bar used during extraction is common on pump-driven units; the extra headroom is there to maintain consistent pressure as the puck resists flow. What matters more for flavor is whether the machine holds pressure steadily, and that isn't something the specs can tell you. You'll find that out on your first few shots. The grinder design is the real story here. A 20% wider polished chute paired with a reinforced helical auger is a direct response to the clogging that plagues budget integrated grinders, especially with oily dark roasts. Wider, smoother surfaces give grounds less to catch on, and a helical auger pushes material through rather than letting it cake. If you drink medium or dark roasts daily, this is the spec that will most affect how often you're cleaning the machine versus using it. The 58mm portafilter is worth flagging. It's the same diameter used on many commercial and prosumer machines, which means aftermarket baskets, tampers, and distribution tools built for that size will generally fit. If you already own 58mm accessories, they carry over. The 2.3-liter removable tank is generous for a home unit. You're not refilling it every morning, and because it detaches, you can fill it at the sink instead of pouring from a pitcher. For everyday listening. The detachable drip tray helps with cleanup, though the specs don't state its capacity. In everyday use, the steam wand handles milk texturing for lattes, cappuccinos, and flat whites. The listing describes it as producing microfoam, but there's no stated wand articulation, number of holes, or steam pressure figure, so how it performs on a single pitcher versus back-to-back pitchers is unknown from the data. Limitations and data gaps Several specs you'd normally want before buying are missing.
