The pitch is straightforward: a 12-inch stainless ruler, an aluminum head with a bubble level, a built-in scriber, and a locking mechanism. It replaces a try square, miter square, depth gauge, straight edge, and spirit level. For a home workshop or job-site bag, consolidating those into one tool saves space.  The laser-etched markings are the strongest point here. Unlike stamped or painted graduations that wear off with use, etching into the stainless steel means the numbers stay legible over time. The dual scales - metric on one side, SAE on the other - cover both measurement systems, which is useful if you switch between European and North American plans. The 1/16 and 1/8 inch increments are standard for framing and rough carpentry; if you need 1/32 or 1/64 precision, this blade won't give it to you. The aluminum head is a deliberate trade-off. Zinc alloy heads are heavier and often more stable when you clamp the square against a workpiece, but they fatigue your hand during extended layout work. Aluminum keeps this unit lighter, which helps portability but may mean less mass holding the head firmly against the ruler edge during repeated marking. The spring-loaded locking screw is a capable design choice - it adjusts smoothly and clamps down without needing a separate wrench. The built-in scriber stores in the head, which is convenient, but the spec doesn't say what steel it's made from or how hard it is. A soft scriber will dull quickly on hardwood or metal. The bubble level is integrated into the head, so you can check plumb and horizontal without grabbing a separate level, though a 12-inch level is short for longer spans. Here's the honest gap: the listing doesn't specify accuracy tolerances. For a combination square, the 90-degree and 45-degree angles need to be dead-on, and the ruler requires to be straight. No spec sheet figure for squareness, straightness, or calibration is provided. That's a meaningful omission for a layout tool where 0.005 inches* (based on available data) of error translates into a visible gap in a joint. You're trusting the manufacturing quality without data to back it up. Also missing is weight. For everyday listening, the listing says aluminum is lighter than zinc, but doesn't give a number. For a tool you'll carry in an apron or pouch, knowing the actual weight matters. And there's no mention of whether the ruler detaches fully for standalone use - the capability list says it does, but the technical specs don't confirm the mechanism. Summary This square suits a woodworker or DIYer who demands one tool for fundamental layout - marking 90s, 45s, checking level, and scribing lines - without carrying four separate instruments. Who this is for: the hobbyist carpenter setting up a compact shop, the DIYer doing weekend shelving and trim work, or the apprentice who wants a single tool for site layout. The scriber and level make it a decent all-in-one for quick marks on studs or plywood. Who should look elsewhere: anyone doing finish work where 1/32-inch accuracy is non-negotiable - fine joinery, instrument cases, or precision metalwork. ### Frequently Asked Questions About the Micymedo 12 Inch

The 12-inch stainless steel blade has 300mm metric markings on one side and SAE 1/16 and 1/8 inch scales on the other, so you can work in either system.

Yes, the ruler detaches from the head for standalone use, and the head stores a metal scriber for marking wood or metal.
