The Firecore 9-Foot Aluminum Grade Rod solves that specific annoyance by printing two different scales on opposite faces of the same rod. The front side is marked in feet and tenths; the back is marked in feet and inches. For everyday listening, the rod itself is aluminum alloy with anodized sections and the graduations are printed in weather- and corrosion-resistant inks. Black and red marks handle the visibility.  The 9-foot extension is the practical ceiling here. If your shots routinely run above 9 feet - tall embankments, deep trenches with a low instrument - you'll top out and need a longer rod. For typical foundation, grading, and flatwork checks, 9 feet covers most of what a level and rod combo will read. Three sections means two joints, and the locking buttons are the weak point on any telescoping rod. The injection-molded joints are spec'd for smooth gliding and the buttons for preventing slippage. Rectangular cross-section keeps the rod from rotating in your hands, which matters when you're trying to keep a face square to the instrument. The anodized finish and corrosion-resistant inks are the right choices for outdoor work where rain and dust are constant. The dual-scale layout earns its keep during solo work. You can read heights at eye level from the front face without walking around, then flip to the inches side for a quick check. Crews that report in decimal feet for cut/fill calculations and inches for as-built notes get both from one stick. What's missing is what you'd want to know before committing: there's no weight listed, no collapsed length, no stated accuracy tolerance or graduation increment spacing. For a grade rod, the graduation resolution - whether you can read to the hundredth of a foot or just the tenth - is the spec that separates a rough-grading tool from a fine-grading one, and the listing doesn't give it. Weight matters too; a 9-foot aluminum rod gets tiring to hold steady on a windy day, and without a number you're guessing. The waterproof bag is a nice inclusion, but "waterproof" vs. "water-resistant" isn't clarified beyond the label. This rod fits the person running a laser or optical level for site layout, grading, or concrete work who demands a dual-scale stick that extends to 9 feet and collapses for transport. A solo contractor checking pad elevations, or a petite crew that reports in both decimal feet and inches, gets the most from the two-faced design. Look elsewhere if you need readings above 9 feet, if your work demands fine graduations finer than tenths (the listing doesn't promise them), or if you need a rod with a confirmed weight and collapsed dimension before you commit.
