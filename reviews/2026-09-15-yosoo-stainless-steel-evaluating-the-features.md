from what I can tell the material spec is the one worth pausing on. 201 stainless steel is a lower-grade alloy than the 304 you'll find in most pitchers sold for coffee work. It has less nickel and more manganese, which makes it cheaper to produce but also less resistant to corrosion over time, particularly if you leave milk residue sitting in it or run it through a dishwasher regularly. For a pitcher that touches acidic coffee and dairy daily, 304 is the usual recommendation. The listing calls 201 "premium" - that's marketing language, not a metallurgical claim, and it doesn't hold up against the grade number printed right next to it. The size range is genuinely useful, though. Six capacities from 250mL up to 2000mL means you can pick based on your actual steaming volume. A 350mL pitcher handles a single 6oz cappuccino with room for the steam wand. The 600mL is the sweet spot for two drinks. In everyday use, the 1000mL and up are really for batch frothing or use as a general-purpose pouring jug in a small cafe or office kitchen. The corresponding weight range of 173g to 680g tracks with that - the smallest is light enough to maneuver one-handed, the largest is heavy enough that you'll notice it after a few pours. The dripless spout and ergonomic handle are the two features that matter for actual latte art. A sharp, tapered spout is what gives you control at the end of a pour, and the listing does specify a dripless design. The handle is described as labor-saving, which is vague, but at the smaller sizes the weight handles that. The lid is a fine inclusion for storage or transport, though it has no role in steaming - you'd remove it before frothing. Two things the data doesn't tell you. There's no base diameter or height listed, only "shown as picture," so you can't confirm whether the 350mL fits under your specific espresso machine's steam wand or group head. And there's no wall thickness spec, which is what determines how the pitcher feels in hand and how well it holds temperature during a pour. Those are the two specs I'd want before buying any pitcher, and neither is here. The listing also notes that modern and established types ship randomly, which means you may not get the exact version pictured. That's worth knowing if the spout shape is the reason you're buying. Summary This pitcher covers a wide capacity range at a low material grade. The 201 stainless steel is the main compromise - functional for milk frothing, but below the 304 standard common in this category. The six size options and included lid make it flexible for home or light commercial use. Anyone prioritizing corrosion resistance or dishwasher safety should weigh that against the grade spec before deciding. Who this is for Someone who wants a single low-cost pitcher for home espresso and isn't concerned about long-term corrosion resistance. If you're making one or two lattes a day, rinsing by hand, and the 350mL or 600mL size fits your machine, the specs line up with the job. It also works for a compact office kitchen where a 1000mL jug gets used for batch frothing and general pouring. Who should look elsewhere If you run a busy cafe, steam multiple pitchers per hour, or put your tools through a dishwasher, 201 steel is the wrong choice. Look for a 304-grade pitcher with a published wall thickness and base diameter - those specs matter more than capacity once you're pouring under time pressure.
